## The short version

The tester liked **tone monitoring** and the faster feedback response. The main problem was the first-time experience: they did not know what to do, could not leave a call, and felt pushed through conversations too fast.

The pattern is clear:

**confusion -> rushed conversation -> harsh feedback -> pressure to keep talking**

For a product meant to help people feel more comfortable speaking, that loop can create the exact stress we want to lower.

## 1. Explain the game before the first call

The tester explored the site without knowing how it worked. They wanted a short intro or walkthrough.

The current prompt also felt too indirect. Instead of telling them to "inform Ross about your startup/work life," the character should respond to what they just said and make the exchange feel like a real conversation.

**Try:** a 30-second guided first run with one clear goal, one example response, and a visible exit button.

## 2. Slow down the characters

The characters spoke very fast. The tester said it gave the game a rushed feeling that could put off anxious or socially awkward players. They thought the speed was fine in the feedback response, so the issue is the live conversation pace.

**Try:** slow character speech during calls, add a short pause before replies, and test a speech-speed setting.

## 3. Make live feedback feel safer

The tone monitor was a standout. But immediate negative feedback while someone is speaking may make them freeze or lose their train of thought.

The tester also questioned why saying "um" is treated as the main sign of poor speaking. Fillers can matter, but they are only one part of communication. Clarity, pace, confidence, listening, and whether the answer fits the moment matter too.

**Try:** keep live feedback light and save the full score for the end. Treat fillers as one signal, not the verdict.

## 4. Rethink the five-minute goal

Five minutes felt unnatural for short scenarios. The tester asked how anyone is supposed to talk to Nick the Neighbor for five minutes straight.

A good neighbor chat may last 45 seconds. A startup pitch may last several minutes. One time goal does not fit every scene.

**Try:** set a target range by scenario and reward a natural ending, not time spent filling space.

## 5. Show the player what happened

The strongest feature idea was a transcript panel, like the review screen on Chess.com. It could mark the moments where the player rushed, used a filler, missed a cue, or gave a strong response.

That would turn a score into something a player can study and retry.

**Simple version:** transcript + timestamp + feedback label + one better response.

## 6. Fix the two broken moments

- **No mid-call exit:** the tester could not end a call while exploring the site. The exit control needs to stay visible and work at any point.
- **Larry dialogue glitch:** Larry said, "ah, good, i have been ... i have been curious about one." It stuttered, and the sentence did not make sense in context.

## Suggested order

| Priority | Change | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Make "end call" work and stay visible | A new player should never feel trapped |
| 2 | Add the first-run walkthrough | Removes the biggest source of confusion |
| 3 | Slow live character speech | Makes the game feel calmer |
| 4 | Move harsh feedback to the recap | Keeps players from freezing mid-answer |
| 5 | Use scenario-based time goals | Makes conversations feel natural |
| 6 | Add transcript review | Gives players a clear way to improve |
| 7 | Fix Larry's line and stutter | Removes a trust-breaking character bug |

## What to keep

- Tone monitoring felt useful and different.
- The feedback response speed felt right.
- The tester wanted more explanation and control, not less feedback.
