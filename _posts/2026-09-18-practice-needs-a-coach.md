## The question

A tester described what it feels like to practice with Mary and the other characters:

> "I'm essentially freestyling or trying to produce what I assume is good speech. I'm often not sure what would actually take me to the next level."

They know the familiar advice: avoid filler words, keep talking, sound confident. But repetition without a clear standard can turn into rehearsing the same habits. They want feedback on coherence, fluency, word choice, grammatical range, and accuracy. In other words, they do not only want a place to speak. They want a coach.

They also asked a fair product question: Moments used to get closer to this. If Moments are no longer visible and the characters now live inside a game, is SpeakEasy moving away from coaching?

## Practice and coaching do different jobs

The game solves an important part of speaking practice: getting the reps in.

A conversation with a character gives the player a reason to respond in the moment. There is an objective, another voice to react to, and enough pressure that the practice does not feel like reading from a worksheet. That matters. Speaking is a live skill, so some of the learning has to happen live.

But the tester is right that practice alone is incomplete. A player can finish ten conversations and still not know what changed, which habit is holding them back, or what to try in conversation eleven.

A useful loop needs all four steps:

1. **Learn:** understand what a stronger response does differently.
2. **Practice:** try it in a real exchange.
3. **Review:** see where the response worked and where it broke down.
4. **Retry:** enter the next conversation with one specific adjustment.

The game is strong at step two. The coaching layer has to make the other three just as clear.

## What the game measures now

SpeakEasy is not only counting filler words. The current conversation scoring system can judge purpose, clarity, word choice, structure, pace, and fillers. After a run, the recap can show the transcript, the score path, and the strongest and weakest takes behind each part of the score, including the reason and the original audio.

That is the beginning of the coach the tester is asking for. It connects a number to something the player actually said.

But the gap is still real. Those categories are not yet presented as a learning path. "You lost points on structure" is a diagnosis. Coaching starts when the product explains the pattern, shows a better move, and gives the player a focused chance to try it again.

The tester's language is also useful. Coherence, fluency, lexical resource, grammatical range, and accuracy are standards people already recognize from serious language assessment. SpeakEasy does not need to copy an exam rubric, but it should be able to answer the same underlying questions:

- Did the response hold together?
- Did it move at a natural pace?
- Were the words precise or repetitive?
- Did the speaker have enough sentence range for the idea?
- Did errors make the meaning harder to follow?

The score should tell the player which of those matters most right now, not flatten all of them into one percentage.

## What happened to Moments

Moments were built around a good idea: isolate the parts of a recording that changed what the listener understood. Instead of giving broad advice about an entire conversation, show the exact sentence or decision that helped or hurt.

That idea has not stopped being useful. It appears in the current product model as the strongest and weakest takes behind the score, with transcript, reason, and playback. But if a returning user cannot find Moments, the implementation does not matter. A coaching feature that is hidden feels like a removed feature.

The lesson is not necessarily to restore the old screen exactly as it was. It is to preserve what made Moments valuable: evidence, specificity, and a clear next move. The recap should make those pieces impossible to miss.

## Is SpeakEasy pivoting to a game?

The honest answer is that the game is becoming the practice environment, not a replacement for coaching.

The characters, objectives, map, score, and progression give people a reason to keep speaking. The coaching system should turn each run into useful instruction. If the game becomes engaging but does not make the player better, it misses the product's purpose. If the coaching is accurate but people do not want to practice, it misses too.

The target is not "game or coach." It is a game that acts like a coach.

## What we should build toward

The next version of the loop should make improvement visible:

1. Before a conversation, give the player one skill to focus on.
2. During the conversation, keep feedback light enough that it does not interrupt speaking.
3. After the conversation, show the exact strongest and weakest moments.
4. Explain the largest pattern across coherence, fluency, word choice, grammar, pace, and fillers.
5. Offer one better version and let the player retry immediately.
6. Track whether that skill improves across later conversations.

This turns Mary and the other characters from virtual friends you freestyle with into practice partners inside a larger coaching system.

The tester's question points to the right standard for SpeakEasy: after every run, a player should know what they learned, what they practiced, and what to do next.
