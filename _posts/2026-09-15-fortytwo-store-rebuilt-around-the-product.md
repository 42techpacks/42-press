title: "The FORTYTWO Store, Rebuilt Around the Product"
date: 2026-09-15
---

Blaze Smith's best point is simple: a store should carry the brand and answer the buyer's questions at the same time. FORTYTWO already has a strong product world. The site should make that world easier to enter, scan, trust, and buy.

## What we watched

I reviewed 20 recent posts from Blaze at [Shovel Studio](https://www.tiktok.com/@shovel.studio). The post Milo sent was his [Brandy Melville collection-page review](https://www.tiktok.com/@shovel.studio/video/7683509473113099550).

His repeated ideas were:

- Use brand feeling to guide the whole store, not only the logo.
- Make the first screen useful. Most people will not explore far.
- Keep product grids consistent and easy to scan.
- Give every product line a clear path from the homepage.
- Use product pages to answer every buyer question.
- Put fit, size, shipping, and checkout controls close to the buy button.
- Use strong campaign images, but do not bury the products.
- Add small moments of motion or play when they fit the release.
- Build a design system so the store stays consistent.
- Test changes. A good-looking store is not finished until it works.

The percentages in his posts are his claims. We should treat them as ideas to test, not promises.

## The problem on FORTYTWO now

The current store opens almost straight into one large product grid. That is good for speed, but the first screen does not explain the label, the current drop, or why these pieces matter.

The store has 29 products. Most are jerseys. The main collection mixes in-stock items, partly sold-out items, and fully sold-out items. Product cards use different amounts and types of photography. Some products have two images. One has ten. That makes the grid feel less planned than the clothes.

The nav is also too thin for the catalog. "SHOP," "FAQ," and "CONTACT" work, but they do not help someone jump to jerseys, polos, tees, or outerwear. The homepage and collection page do almost the same job.

The product pages need more proof. A shopper should not have to guess about fit, knit weight, feel, care, shipping time, or how the piece looks on a body.

## The new direction

The store should feel like a digital football program crossed with a clean fashion catalog.

Keep the page fast and direct. Use one strong campaign frame, clean type, fine rules, and tight product grids. Add small details from match graphics: squad lists, country codes, season marks, and player-card data. Do not turn the site into a sports game. The clothes stay first.

### Design rules

- Use one type system across the full store.
- Use one spacing scale and one border weight.
- Use off-white, black, and one changing accent color per drop.
- Keep buttons solid, large, and clear.
- Keep motion short. Use it to explain state, not to decorate empty space.
- Use the same image ratio on every product card.
- Give every release a campaign rule: one hero style, one studio style, one detail style, and one on-body style.

## Priority 1: Fix the path to product

### 1. Give the homepage a real first screen

Show one strong campaign image for the live drop. Add a short line that says what it is. Add one solid button: "SHOP THE DROP."

Keep product cards visible at the bottom of the first screen on desktop. On mobile, let the next section peek into view. The page should feel shoppable before the first scroll.

### 2. Put every product line on the homepage

After the hero, add clear entries for:

- Knitted jerseys
- Polos
- Tees
- Knit outerwear

Use a strong image and one sentence for each. Do not use overlapping copy or stacked promos. Each section gets one job.

### 3. Turn "SHOP" into useful routes

The menu should open to:

- New
- Jerseys
- Polos
- Tees
- Outerwear
- All products
- Sold out archive

The catalog is small enough to stay simple. It is large enough to need these paths.

### 4. Clean up the collection page

Use four columns on desktop and two on mobile. Keep all card images at one ratio. Show product name, price, color or country, and available sizes without opening the item.

Add quick filters for category, country, size, price, and availability. Put in-stock items first by default. Move fully sold-out pieces into an archive unless a restock is planned.

Insert one large editorial image after the second desktop row. It can show a campaign frame, a fan photo, or a close knit detail. Then return to products.

## Priority 2: Rebuild the product page

The product page is where most of the work should happen.

### First screen

- Large on-body image
- Product name and price
- One-line product truth
- Size buttons, not a dropdown
- Clear sold-out states
- Solid "ADD TO CART" button
- Short shipping estimate
- Link to size help

Keep these controls visible while the customer moves through the first images.

### Image set for every product

Set a minimum six-frame shot list:

1. Front on body
2. Back on body
3. Front flat
4. Back flat
5. Knit or print close-up
6. Styled vertical video

Add one wider campaign frame when it exists. On mobile, show four strong images before any hidden gallery. Do not make buyers swipe through ten nearly equal frames.

### Fit and size

Add a visual size guide to every item. Include inches and centimeters. Show model height, model size, and the intended fit.

Add a short "How it fits" note. For example: "Boxy through the body. Cropped at the waist. Size up for a loose match-day fit."

Later, add a fit finder based on height, weight, and preferred fit. Start with a plain chart first. Good data matters more than a fancy tool.

### Product proof

Use short blocks, not one long text wall:

- Fit
- Fabric and knit weight
- Construction details
- Care
- Shipping and returns
- Made in / production note

Show why the piece costs what it costs. For the knit jerseys, zoom in on the weave, collar, badge, and hem. Put the build of the garment on the page.

### How to style it

Add one 10- to 20-second phone-style video. Show one piece in two or three fits. This can use FORTYTWO's own team, friends, players, or customers. It should feel real, not like a polished ad.

### End of page

End with related pieces and a clean path back to the collection. Do not leave the shopper at a dead end.

## Priority 3: Make the brand system feel owned

### 1. Build a small visual system from football culture

Create a set of parts that can repeat without looking copied:

- Country and club codes
- Season and match numbers
- Small grid patterns from nets and stadium seating
- A score-strip style for product facts
- A simple sound cue for campaign video, used only when sound is on

Use these parts across the site, packaging, email, and launch posts. This is how the store becomes part of the brand, not a skin around Shopify.

### 2. Use one memorable interaction per drop

For a tournament release, a small fixture picker could route shoppers by country. For a club release, a squad-board interaction could reveal the pieces. A short penalty or passing game could unlock a lookbook.

Only build one if it leads to product. It should work with touch, load fast, and have a clear skip button.

### 3. Add an archive

Sold-out work should move into a visual archive instead of clogging the live shop. The archive can show the year, drop, campaign, and product story. It gives the brand history without confusing the checkout path.

## What not to copy

Do not copy the empty celebrity-store model. FORTYTWO cannot rely on fame to replace fit and shipping information.

Do not hide key items low on the page to force more scrolling. Do not use random motion. Do not add glass effects or unusual scrims unless they help the current campaign. Do not borrow another brand's exact layout, font mix, or interaction.

The lesson is not to make the site busier. It is to make each detail feel chosen.

## Build plan

### Week 1: Measure and set the system

- Record current conversion rate, add-to-cart rate, checkout rate, average order value, mobile bounce, and top exits.
- Group all products by category, country, stock state, and release.
- Set the type, color, spacing, border, button, card, and image rules.
- Write the six-frame photo list and PDP copy template.

### Week 2: Homepage and collection

- Build the campaign hero.
- Add category routes.
- Rework the grid and filters.
- Add the editorial card.
- Move fully sold-out items to the archive.

### Week 3: Product-page template

- Build size buttons, fit guide, shipping note, product facts, and sticky buy controls.
- Shoot the missing on-body, detail, and style content for the top sellers first.
- Apply the new template to five top products.

### Week 4: Test and roll out

- Test the new homepage against the current direct grid.
- Test the new PDP on top traffic products.
- Watch mobile recordings for missed images, size confusion, and dead taps.
- Fix the template, then move the rest of the catalog over.

## Test order

Run one major test at a time:

1. Campaign hero plus visible products vs. the current grid-first homepage
2. New PDP vs. the current PDP
3. On-body first image vs. flat first image
4. Size buttons vs. the current selector
5. Shipping estimate near the buy button vs. shipping details lower down
6. In-stock-first sorting vs. the current order

Judge each test by add-to-cart rate and completed orders. Keep an eye on average order value and returns. A lift in clicks is not enough if fit confusion causes more returns.

## The first version to ship

If we only do one pass, ship this:

- One campaign-led homepage with products still visible early
- Category links for jerseys, polos, tees, and outerwear
- A clean four-by-two product grid
- In-stock-first sorting
- A rebuilt PDP with six useful media frames
- Size buttons, a visual fit chart, model info, and shipping estimate
- A short style video
- A sold-out archive

That is the right base. The interactive football ideas can come after the store becomes easier to shop.
